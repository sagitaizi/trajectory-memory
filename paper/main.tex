\section{Introduction}
Sight is by far the dominant sense humans use to perceive the world around them. The retina, a thin sheet of tissue in the eye, converts raw light into nerve signals that we then interpret as visual images. However, a flash of light can evoke a neural response in the brain with a delay of 30--100 ms \cite{Berry1999Anticipation}, during which a moving target can cover a significant distance and should therefore be seen behind its actual position. As this contradicts our daily experience, it has been suggested that the brain acts as a `prediction machine', modelling what comes next and attending to what deviates \cite{Clark2013Whatever}. This is most striking for repetitive motion, where the eyes can lock on to a moving target within a few cycles and follow it with almost no lag \cite{Barnes1991The}.

The retina itself has a silicon counterpart \cite{Mahowald1991The}. Event cameras are low-power sensors that sample light based on the scene dynamics, and report per-pixel brightness changes as a stream of events with microsecond latency \cite{Gallego2022Event}. Spiking neural networks (SNNs) mimic the neurons behind it, computing via asynchronous discrete spikes which are sparse in time and allow for lower energy consumption and use with noisy inputs. Yet most pipelines built using them for tracking purposes react rather than anticipate---locating the target and extrapolating its position. A target that repeats its motion is exactly the case where prediction is possible, and event cameras and SNNs together are the parts from which such a biologically inspired prediction machine can be built. Such a system is useful in any application where a quick signal indicating that a target has deviated from its expected path is needed, for example in industrial inspection, robotics, and autonomous vehicles \cite{Sho2026A,Thoduka2021Using}. 

A target oscillating in a repetitive manner generates events in a periodic pattern. The task at hand is to learn this pattern from the event stream alone, predict the target's position a short horizon ahead, and signal when the motion stops matching. Real object movement, however, is never perfect--period and amplitude can drift and events can carry sensor noise, so the system must distinguish between these natural variations and actual deviation. For real-world scenarios, learning and detection must be performed online and without prior knowledge of the target's motion.

Separate parts of this problem have been previously addressed. Classical periodic filters like the Kalman filter \cite{Kalman1960A} predict repetitive motion well, but they assume a clean stream of accurate positions and are not spiking by nature. SNNs on event cameras have been used to predict trajectories, but primarily for one-shot motions and without a deviation signal (Section~\ref{sec:related}).

In this work, we propose a spiking system for online prediction built in two stages and tested on real-world cases using a real event camera. The first, a spiking localiser, estimates the target's position from short windows (5~ms) of accumulated events. Implemented with snnTorch \cite{Eshraghian2022Training}, it is trained with backpropagation through time (BPTT) using surrogate gradients \cite{Neftci2019Surrogate} on simulated clips and kept fixed. The second, a spiking memory built with the Neural Engineering Framework in Nengo \cite{Eliasmith2003Neural,Bekolay2014Nengo}, uses the localiser's output to learn the target's trajectory online. It relies on the fact that a repetitive motion with a defined cycle can be described by two things--where the target is at each point of the cycle--a map, and where in the cycle the target is now--a clock. The clock is an adaptive-frequency oscillator that locks on to the motion's rhythm \cite{Righetti2006Dynamic}, and the map is learned online on top of it. Once the map has settled, a copy of it is kept as the remembered path and updated only slowly from then on, and a deviation is flagged when the target moves too far from it.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item To our knowledge, the first spiking trajectory memory for event-camera input: a clock and a map of phase cells that learn a repeating path online and predict it ahead.
\item A deviation signal from the same memory, which flags when the motion departs from the learned path within a fraction of the cycle time, and fires no false alarms on steady motion.
\item An evaluation on real event-camera recordings of repetitive motions against classical periodic baselines, with a localiser trained on simulation only.
\end{itemize}
The rest of the paper is organised as follows--Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the method, and Section~\ref{sec:setup} the experimental setup. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results, which are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Related work on trajectory prediction from event cameras is mostly focused on single, one-shot motions. Debat et al.~\cite{Debat2021Event} trained an SNN with unsupervised spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) to predict where a thrown ball will land. More recent work predicts the flight of a ping-pong ball in real time from an event camera worn on-head \cite{Alberico2025Egocentric}, with a fitted trajectory prediction model and no spiking network. Neither works involve building a memory of a repeating path nor reporting when motion changes.

Repetitive motion has been mostly modelled outside of vision. Structural time-series models track a repeating pattern as a set of rotating harmonics within a Kalman filter \cite{Harvey2002Forecasting}, but take the period as a known. Since the period is not known in our task, and as we have used this model as a baseline in our work, our implementation estimates it from the first few seconds of motion and otherwise runs it unchanged (whereas our memory finds and follows the period online). Gams et al.~\cite{Gams2009On} learn a periodic movement with no knowledge of its period by pairing an adaptive-frequency oscillator with a waveform learned online. This is the design closest to ours, but it is not spiking and learns from a robot's joints signals rather than vision.

Detecting deviations as errors of prediction is also common in other settings. Video anomaly detection predicts the next frame and flags frames it predicts poorly \cite{Xu2022Motion}, and a robot's task can be monitored by memorizing the optical flow of a successful execution and comparing it to the observed one \cite{Thoduka2021Using}. In our system, the normal motion is learned online, and the deviation is read from the same memory that predicts.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Our proposed system is a spiking pipeline built from two stages of SNNs as seen in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}. Events from the camera are accumulated into short 5ms frames from which a spiking localiser estimates the target's position in the scene. Given this constant stream of positions, the trajectory memory SNN learns the target's path, predicts where the target will be a short time ahead, and flags when motion departs from the estimated path. The localiser is trained once on simulated data only and kept fixed, while the memory is not trained in advance and learns online. Section~\ref{sec:problem} defines the task while the event input, localiser and memory, including its deviation score, are described in Sections~\ref{sec:input}--\ref{sec:memory}.

\subsection{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:problem}
A static event camera is placed in front of an object moving along a certain path. Let $p(t)$ be the target's actual position in the image at time $t$ in the camera frame ($640\times480$ pixels). Motion is repetitive--$p(t+nT) \approx p(t)$ for a period $T$ which is not known in advance. Events are generated by the target's motion and arrive as a stream of asynchronous brightness changes.

At every time step $t$ the system must produce two outputs from this event stream: a prediction $\hat{p}(t+h)$ of the target's position a fixed horizon $h$ ahead ranging from 25 to 200~ms, with $h = 100$~ms as the main one, and a scalar deviation score $s(t)$. A deviation is defined as a change in the target's motion after which the learned path is considered no longer followed, such as a push that changes the amplitude or direction of the swing or a change in speed. A high deviation score suggests such a change has occured and the system must alert it as fast as possible.

\subsection{Event Input}
\label{sec:input}
All real events are captured with a DVXplorer event camera with a resolution of $640\times480$ pixels. Each event $e=(x, y, t, q)$ is a single brightness change at pixel $(x, y)$ at time $t$, with polarity $q$ marking an increase (ON) or a decrease (OFF). The stream is divided into consecutive windows of 5~ms each accumulated into a frame of two channels, one for ON and one for OFF events at every pixel. The frames are then reduced by $8\times$ by summing $8\times8$ pixels blocks, giving a $2\times60\times80$ (channels $\times$ height $\times$ width) input size for every 5~ms step. The window length sets how often the system produces an output and how long it waits for its input, and is the main latency parameter of the pipeline.

\subsection{Spiking Localiser}
\label{sec:localiser}
Given the event frames, the localiser is responsible for locating the target in each one of them (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, Stage~1). It is a convolutional spiking network where each layer is composed of feature maps--grids of leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons. A LIF neuron (as used in our localiser implementation) $i$ sums its input current $I_i$ into a membrane potential $U_i$ that leaks, and spikes when $U_i$ crosses a threshold $\theta$, after which $\theta$ is subtracted~\cite{Eshraghian2022Training}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
U_i[t] &= \beta_i U_i[t-1] + I_i[t] - \theta S_i[t-1],\\
S_i[t] &= \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } U_i[t] > \theta\\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_i = e^{-\Delta t/\tau_i}$ marks the leak, with $\Delta t = 5$~ms and $\tau_i$ spread over 10--20~ms. This leak gives the network a natural memory of the last few frames, so neurons can remain active and keep responding to the target through frames in which it produces few events.

Each neuron receives its input current as a weighted sum of the activity in a small patch of the layer below. All neurons of one map use the same set of weights, called a filter, so that each map detects exactly one pattern and shows where in the input it appears. The first layer has 16 feature maps of $30\times40$ neurons, each seeing $5\times5$ input blocks ($40\times40$ pixels) with a stride of 2, which halves the grid. A single map from this layer mainly responds to local patterns of ON and OFF events, such as the edges of a moving object. The second has 32 maps of $15\times20$, each neuron seeing $13\times13$ blocks ($104\times104$ pixels) with a stride of 2 as well, which is usually enough to hold the entire target or a large part of it. A fully connected layer of 256 LIF neurons then sees the whole image at once, where evidence from all positions can be compared to decide where the target is.

The position readout is inspired by place cells \cite{OKeefe1971The}, neurons in the hippocampus of various animals (including humans) that fire mainly when the animal is physically present at one specific location. In our implementation, each axis is covered by 32 cells, each tuned to one position $c_k$, so a position is represented by a bump of activity over the few cells tuned nearest to it (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} Stage 1). To compute the cells' activities $z_k$, the spikes of the fully connected layer are smoothed with a 15~ms low-pass filter and summed with learned weights. The position is then defined as the centre of mass of the bump, with each cell weighted by the softmax of its activity,
\begin{equation}
\hat{x} = \sum_k c_k \frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}},
\end{equation}
and likewise for $\hat{y}$, giving the estimate $\tilde{p}(t) = (\hat{x}, \hat{y})$ that the memory receives.
One more output tells whether the target is visible; when it is not, the step reaches the memory as unseen.

The network is trained on simulated recordings only (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) with a loss function aiming to match a Gaussian bump one cell wide centred on the true position and a penalty that keeps the fully connected layer spiking on about 10\% of steps. Camera effects the simulator does not produce, such as background noise, brightness changes and short gaps in the target's events, are added to the frames during training. The network is then fine-tuned on simulated pendulum motion and kept fixed.

\subsection{Trajectory-Memory Network}
\label{sec:memory}
The memory receives the localiser's estimate $\tilde{p}(t)$ every 5~ms and outputs a prediction $\hat{p}(t+h)$ and a deviation score (Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}, Stage~2). A repeating motion can be fully described by where the target is at each \emph{phase} of its cycle, and can be translated to a trajectory given a connection between the phase and the target's position. The memory therefore consists of two main components: a clock that follows the phase, and a map that learns online the target's position at each phase.

\emph{Clock.} The clock is a variant of the adaptive-frequency oscillator seen in \cite{Righetti2006Dynamic}, an oscillator that synchronizes with a rhythmic input and learns its frequency. Its input $F(t)$ is the target's position projected on the main axis of motion, found online from a running estimate of the positions' covariance, and scaled to a unit amplitude. The clock's state $(x_c, y_c)$ turns around a unit circle at a rate $\omega$ while $F$ pulls on its phase $\varphi$ and, more slowly, on its rate:
\begin{align}
\dot{x}_c &= \gamma(1-r^2)\,x_c - \omega\,(1-\kappa F y_c)\,y_c, \nonumber\\
\dot{y}_c &= \gamma(1-r^2)\,y_c + \omega\,(1-\kappa F y_c)\,x_c, \label{eq:clock}\\
\dot{\omega} &= -\eta_\omega\,\omega F y_c, \nonumber
\end{align}
with $r^2 = x_c^2 + y_c^2$, $\varphi = \mathrm{atan2}(y_c, x_c)$, and the constants $\gamma = 2$, $\kappa = 0.3$ and $\eta_\omega = 0.6$. The first term of each of the first two equations pulls the state back to the unit circle each step, and the second turns it around the circle at a rate of $\omega(1-\kappa F y_c)$, so that $F$ either speeds the clock up or slows it down depending on where it is in its cycle. The last equation applies the same product $F y_c$ to $\omega$ itself--when the clock runs ahead of its input, $F y_c$ is positive on average and $\omega$ decreases, and when it runs behind, $\omega$ increases. After a few cycles the rate syncs with the frequency of the motion and the phase keeps a fixed relation to it, so $\varphi$ tells how far through its cycle the target is.

The clock's state $(x_c, y_c)$ together with $\omega$ and $F$ were represented by a population of 1,500 LIF neurons, and the recurrent connections that make the population follow \eqref{eq:clock} were built using the Neural Engineering Framework in Nengo. Nengo solves the weights by least squares, so that the neurons' activity fed back through their synapses reproduces \eqref{eq:clock}. The rate itself is updated outside the population and fed back into it, since holding it as a neural integrator made it drift slightly. Since one clock will only sync with periods near its own, six clocks, started at periods between 0.8~s and 7~s, are run simultaneously. After 3~s of motion, the clock whose period is closest to the motion's period measured roughly from the times $F$ crosses zero is used. The clock's final outputs then fed to the map are its phase $\varphi$ and its rate $\omega$.

\emph{Map.} The phase $\varphi$ is stored for the map to read as phase cells, which follow the idea of the place cells of the localiser (Section~\ref{sec:localiser}): while a place cell fires when the target is at one location, a phase cell fires when the motion is at one point of its cycle. The phase cells are a ring of 400 LIF neurons, each tuned to one direction on the unit circle and receiving the phase as a unit vector $(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$. At any given step, the cells tuned near $\varphi$ fire, so their activity $a(t)$, which is the smoothed spiking of the 400 cells, is a bump that travels around the ring as the phase advances and marks where in the cycle the target is. The map is a set of connections $W$ from the phase cells to the target's position. It starts at zero in every run and is learned by the error-driven PES rule \cite{MacNeil2011Fine}, normalised by the activity's power rather than the number of neurons:
\begin{equation}
\Delta W = \eta\,\frac{\big(\tilde{p}(t) - W a(t)\big)\,a(t)^{\top}}{\lVert a(t)\rVert^{2}},
\end{equation}
where the learning rate $\eta$ decays from 0.3 to 0.02 over the first few seconds of motion. After a few cycles $W$ holds the target's path, one position per phase. Two further mechanisms keep the map in line with a real target. First, The map's output is stretched or shrunk slowly around its center, so it follows gradual imperfections of real life repetitive paths, without relearning every phase. Second, once a clock is chosen, a position much further than usual from where the map expects the target is ignored, so a momentary error of the localiser does not affect the map.

\emph{Prediction.} To predict a horizon of $h$ ahead, the phase is advanced by $\omega h$ and the map is read through $\bar{a}(\varphi + \omega h)$, the activity the ring would have at the advanced phase:
\begin{equation}
\hat{p}(t+h) = W\,\bar{a}(\varphi + \omega h) + e^{-h/\tau_r}\,\rho(t),
\end{equation}
where $\rho(t)$ is the recent error of the map, smoothed over 20~ms. The second term lets the prediction follow a target that is momentarily off its path, and its weight $e^{-h/\tau_r}$, with $\tau_r = 2$~s, makes its influence fade the further ahead the prediction is.

The neurons of the clock and the ring use Nengo's LIF model (membrane time constant 20~ms, refractory period 2~ms). Rather than in Nengo's own simulator, they run in PyTorch with the weights Nengo solved, so that the memory is stepped frame by frame in the same loop as the localiser and the steps computed alongside it, with the six clocks as one batch.

\emph{Deviation.} Measured against the active map, a deviation would fade within a few cycles as the map constantly learns the new motion. The score is therefore measured against a slow copy $W_s$ of the map, taken once memory has settled. The copy moves towards the working map a little a step: quickly at first, while the map is still settling, and more slowly as the copy ages. A slow drift of the path is thus still followed, while during a deviation the copy barely changes, so the score stays high for as long as the it lasts. The final deviation score is the distance between the target and where the copy expects it, $s(t) = \lVert \tilde{p}(t) - W_s\,a(t) \rVert$, averaged over the last 0.2~s.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Live Runs}
\label{sec:runs}
Every run performed is recorded with the DV software and the event stream is replayed as if live, so that every localiser and memory receives exactly the same input. None are ever trained on. All 19 live runs last 25--30~s and come from three setups, each with two runs that contain a deviation. \emph{Pendulum}: a swinging target hung from a string, with a period of about 1.2~s that swings 170--370~px from end to end. Nine runs, the deviation being a push that changes the swing. \emph{Rotating arm}: a target tied to the blade of a ceiling fan, creating a near-perfect repetitive trajectory. Six runs. \emph{Hand-moved}: a printed black square on a white background (for event contrast) moved by hand along a wall, for example a horizontal sweep of about 150~px repeated every 1.8~s, or a diagonal sweep that turns horizontal as its deviation. Four runs.

On nine runs (six pendulum, one rotating-arm and two hand-moved) the target's position is marked by hand and linearly interpolated for trajectory comparison. On every run with a deviation, its timing is marked by hand.

\subsection{Event-Camera Simulator}
\label{sec:sim}
Simulated recordings are made with the v2e event-camera simulator \cite{Hu2020V2e}. A target is rendered moving along a known path through our camera's calibrated lens model and converted to events. The path is predetermined, so the ground truth for the loss evaluation is exact. The simulator was matched to a live run on five event statistics, all within 17\%. The main corpus holds 320 recordings on five path families (circle, ellipse, figure-eight, Lissajous and straight sweep) with periods of 0.8--4~s, each with its own small camera and target adjustments, and about half with a scripted deviation. A second corpus of 100 recordings renders a target swinging like a real pendulum. The simulated recordings are used to train the localiser, as described in Section~\ref{sec:localiser}.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
\emph{Classical centroid.} As a classical alternative to the spiking localiser, the position is taken as the mean of the events in the most event-dense $16\times16$~px cells of each frame. It is the input of every memory on the rotating-arm and hand-moved runs, and replaces the spiking localiser in the input ablation.

\emph{Memory.} Three classical memories receive the same positions as ours. The \emph{periodic Kalman filter} \cite{Kalman1960A} describes each coordinate as a mean plus three sinusoids, at the motion's frequency and its multiples \cite{Harvey2002Forecasting}. At every step it advances the sinusoids to forecast where the target should be, then corrects them by part of the miss. To predict, it advances them $h$ ahead. The \emph{harmonic fit} finds the mean and three sinusoids that best match the positions of the last three periods (by least squares) and extends that curve $h$ ahead. The \emph{constant-velocity} extrapolator simply draws a straight line through the positions of the last 0.1~s and continues it.

Each scores a deviation by how `surprising' a new position is against its own normal error: the Kalman filter compares the miss of its forecast with the uncertainty it assigns to that forecast (the squared miss over its predicted variance). The harmonic fit compares the new position's distance from its curve with the median distance of the positions the curve was fitted to. The extrapolator compares the new position's distance from its line with the average of that distance over the last 5~s. Each score is smoothed over 0.1~s. The clock-and-map memory itself, written as plain arithmetic rather than spiking neurons, serves as a fourth comparator (Section~\ref{sec:res-ablations}).

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
When evaluating, every memory receives the same positions from the same localiser, and is not given the period. Prediction errors are measured from 6~s into a run (for memory stabilization) until any deviation. Each prediction is compared with a reference position. On labelled runs this is the hand label. On the rotating-arm and hand-moved runs, the hand label and the centroid sit on different points of the target, a fixed distance apart; that distance is subtracted before scoring. On runs without labels, the reference is the localiser's output $h$ later.

Each memory raises an alarm when its deviation score stays above an adaptive threshold: the score's median over the last 2~s plus $k$ times its spread. The threshold is never allowed to rise, so a deviation cannot lift it before it is caught. $k$ is the same for every memory, 25 with the centroid and 30 with the spiking localiser. The system was developed on six of the runs, where these values and the rate of the memory's slow copy were chosen.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
The \emph{prediction error} is the distance in pixels between $\hat{p}(t+h)$ and the reference position at $t+h$. The \emph{path error} compares the memory's remembered cycle at each step with one cycle of the reference path, as the mean distance between the two. Both are a median over the scored steps of each run. For deviation detection, the \emph{AUC} measures how well the score separates the steps after the deviation starts from those before it (1 is perfect separation, 0.5 is chance), the \emph{latency} is the time from the start of the deviation to the first alarm, and \emph{false alarms} are counted per minute before the deviation.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Localiser}
\label{sec:res-localiser}
On the pendulum runs, the spiking localiser places the target nearly five times closer to the labelled point than the classical centroid (Fig.~\ref{fig:localiser}). Over the six labelled pendulum runs its median distance from the label is 14~px (90th percentile 28~px), against 65~px (125~px) for the centroid, which often settles on a different part of the target than the one marked. With each run's constant offset substracted, the spiking localiser holds 8--13~px, against 8--40~px for the centroid. On the rotating arm and the hand-moved target, for which the localiser was not fine-tuned, it still stays within 10--18~px of the label once each run's offset is subtracted, and the memory still works from it (Section~\ref{sec:res-ablations}). The centroid is closer there (5 against 10~px on the rotating arm, 10--17 against 16--18~px on the hand-moved target), so the results that follow use it as the input on these two setups.

\subsection{Prediction and Path Memory}
\label{sec:res-prediction}
The spiking memory predicts the pendulum as well as the classical periodic filters at short horizons, without being given the period (Table~\ref{tab:prediction}, Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon}). Over the six labelled pendulum runs, through the full spiking pipeline, its median error 50~ms ahead is 17.2~px, against 17.5~px for the periodic Kalman filter and 16.6~px for the harmonic fit, and at 100~ms it is 1 and 3~px behind them (20.2 against 19.1 and 17.1~px). At 200~ms it reaches 27.4~px, against 19.1 and 17.5~px. Every periodic memory is far ahead of a constant-velocity extrapolator, whose error at 200~ms, 93~px, is 3.4 times the spiking memory's.

The memory reaches its steady error about 6~s into a run, some five swings and about a second after the baselines finish their 5~s warm-up, and its clock settles within 4\% of the true period on every pendulum run. Its remembered path matches the labelled one to a median of 20~px (Kalman 18~px, harmonic fit 16~px), and it holds the shape of the swing (Fig.~\ref{fig:paths}). On the rotating arm and the hand-moved target, through the classical centroid, the spiking memory predicts within 3~px of the Kalman filter up to 100~ms (11.1 and 15.3 against 10.0 and 12.3~px) and well ahead of the harmonic fit (24.7~px at 100~ms). On one hand-moved run the clock locks on to half the period of the motion, and there the remembered path is furthest from the labelled one (26~px).

On the ten runs without position labels, where each memory is scored against the positions it receives (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), the spiking memory is level with the periodic filters 50~ms ahead (20.0~px, against 20.1~px for both), ahead of the Kalman filter at 100~ms (24.8 against 26.7~px), and behind the harmonic fit (20.5~px). Its clock finds the period to within 5\% on every one of these runs, where the period the baselines estimate from the first 5~s is two and three times the true one on two of them. Its largest errors are on the rotating arm, a median of 39.6~px at 100~ms over five runs, against 30.4~px for the Kalman filter.

\subsection{Deviation Detection}
\label{sec:res-deviation}
Deviation detection is where the spiking memory stands out (Table~\ref{tab:deviation}, Fig.~\ref{fig:break}). It detects all six deviations recorded across the three setups, with a median AUC of 0.97 and a median latency of 0.32~s, and over all 19 runs it raises a single alarm before a deviation. That alarm, on a hand-moved run (Fig.~\ref{fig:break}b), fires 0.27~s before the hand-marked time of the deviation, at the moment the tracked position starts to leave its earlier path, and stays on through the deviation: the memory flagged the deviation before we had marked it by eye. Table~\ref{tab:deviation} scores it as a false alarm, with the deviation's timing left as marked. No other memory detects them all: the arithmetic clock-and-map detects five, at 6.7 false alarms per minute, the periodic Kalman filter three, at 36 per minute and up to 108 on one run, and the harmonic fit and the constant-velocity extrapolator none. On the two pendulum pushes the memory raises its alarm after 0.07 and 0.32~s, against 0.67 and 0.16~s for the Kalman filter; its slowest detections are on the rotating arm, after 1.6 and 5.5~s. On these two runs the target turns faint once per turn and both localisers move onto the string it hangs from, so the score is already high on steady motion and the alarms come late.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:res-ablations}
% CITE: Voelker2019Legendre missing from refs.bib -- Voelker, Kajic & Eliasmith, "Legendre Memory Units", NeurIPS 2019.
\emph{Learned memories.} The two memories that learn the cycle as network dynamics, a recurrent network of fast and slow LIF layers trained on simulated motion and a spiking Legendre Memory Unit \cite{Voelker2019Legendre} that records a window of the past, do not hold the cycle (Fig.~\ref{fig:paths}). On three pendulum runs their remembered paths are 95 and 64~px from the labelled one, with periods 1.89 and 1.26 times the true one, and their 100~ms errors are 32 and 45~px against 23~px for the clock-and-map memory.

\emph{Spiking against arithmetic.} Built from spiking neurons, the clock-and-map memory predicts the pendulum within 1.2~px of its arithmetic form at 50 and 100~ms (17.2 against 16.3~px, 20.2 against 19.0~px) and within 3~px at 200~ms, and it detects all six deviations where the arithmetic form detects five, without its 6.7 false alarms per minute; the arithmetic form flags its detections sooner (median 0.16 against 0.32~s).

\emph{Input.} With the classical centroid in place of the spiking localiser, the spiking memory's 100~ms error on the pendulum rises from a median of 20~px (11--32~px per run) to 63~px (27--130~px), mostly through the centroid's constant offset from the marked point (Section~\ref{sec:res-localiser}): on this setup the localiser sets the accuracy the whole pipeline can reach. In the other direction, with the spiking localiser as input on the rotating arm and the hand-moved target, the spiking memory's 100~ms error rises from 15.3 to 17.8~px and the Kalman filter's from 12.3 to 23.7~px; the spiking memory still detects the hand-moved deviation (AUC 0.98, 0.40~s) without a false alarm, where the Kalman filter raises 46 per minute.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\emph{Prediction and detection.} At short horizons the memory predicts as well as the classical periodic filters, and its error grows faster than theirs with the horizon: reading the map ahead multiplies any error in the clock's rate by the horizon, the clock settles up to 4\% short of the true period on the pendulum, and the spiking neurons add to this error at the longest horizon (Section~\ref{sec:res-ablations}). The deviation signal is where the design pays off. The slow copy of the map keeps the path as first learned and gives the score a stable reference, so the memory detects every deviation and stays quiet on steady motion, while the Kalman filter, whose score measures each step against its own short-term forecast, raises false alarms on 14 of the 19 runs, up to 108 per minute.

\emph{What is spiking.} The localiser, the clock's state and the phase cells are spiking, and the map is a set of learned weights read from them, but several steps around the memory are computed conventionally (Section~\ref{sec:memory}). They are low-dimensional and change over seconds, the kind of work a neuromorphic system leaves to the processor beside the chip; on our CPU, adding them as neural populations would also cost time in a step that already uses most of its 5~ms. Each is a simple function of a few variables, of the kind the Neural Engineering Framework can build into LIF populations, so the memory can be made fully spiking without changing its design. With the localiser and the memory running side by side on a laptop CPU (Intel Core i7-12650H), a 5~ms step takes 4.0--4.4~ms on average, so the pipeline keeps up with the camera in real time. The pipeline is also sparse: on the pendulum, only 2.5--11\% of the localiser's neurons fire in a 5~ms step, and it performs 0.3--0.8 million synaptic operations per step, 9--25 times fewer than the 7.3 million multiply-accumulates of the same network run as a conventional one; the memory's 11,400 LIF neurons fire about 5,400 spikes per step. These counts indicate the saving to expect on neuromorphic hardware, where energy and latency were not measured, as none was available to us.

\emph{Limitations and future work.} The evaluation covers 19 real runs with six deviations, two per setup, so the latencies describe these runs rather than a distribution. The localiser was fine-tuned on simulated pendulum motion and does not improve on the classical centroid for the rotating arm and the hand-moved target; on the rotating arm the memory's detections took 1.6 and 5.5~s, and on one hand-moved run its clock locked on to half the period of the motion. The memory also learns a single path and keeps it: re-learning a new path after a deviation, following several targets at once, and a localiser that reads the raw events without framing them are left for future work.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented a spiking system that learns a repeating trajectory online from an event camera, predicts the target's position a short horizon ahead, and flags when the motion departs from the learned path. A spiking localiser, trained on simulated data only, reads the target's position from 5~ms event frames through place cells, and a spiking memory, built from a clock and a map of phase cells, learns the path within a few cycles without any pretraining. On real event-camera recordings of three repetitive motions, the full pipeline runs in real time on a laptop CPU and predicts the pendulum 50~ms ahead as accurately as classical periodic filters (17~px). Across the three setups it detects all six recorded deviations, the pendulum pushes after 0.07 and 0.32~s, and in 19 runs raises a single alarm before a deviation, 0.27~s ahead of its hand-marked start; the periodic Kalman filter detects three and raises 36 false alarms per minute. These results show that event cameras and spiking networks together can anticipate repetitive motion and notice when it changes, a step towards neuromorphic systems that predict rather than react.
